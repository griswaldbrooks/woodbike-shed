# If it doesn't fit — troubleshooting

> **Goal:** when a part fights you, find the likely cause, fix it to a stated tolerance, or make the honest call and recut the part.

Kit instructions never need a page like this, because a kit is deterministic. A shed built
from real lumber on real ground is not. Wood crowns, ground settles, tapes sag. Work each
entry the same way: read the symptom, check the likely cause against what you actually see,
then fix it — and where the fix says recut, recut. Forcing a bad part in with more fasteners
is how a shed starts to leak and rack.

The crossed-out frames on this page show the **error state** — the red ring and bar mean
"what you must not ship." Where a fix has a number, that number is the tolerance; past it,
the part or the assembly is wrong, not the measurement.

## 1. Crowned or warped stock

**Symptom.** A board bows along its length (crown), twists like a propeller, or bows across
its width. A crowned stud will not sit flat between its plates; a twisted rafter rocks on
its seats.

**Likely cause.** Kiln-dried stock moves after it leaves the kiln, and it moves more if it
was stacked flat in the sun or banded tight off a wet pile. The defect was probably in the
board when you bought it.

**Fix.** Sight down every board over the top edge before it is cut — crown up to ¼″ over
8′ is usable in horizontal members (crown up, load flattens it) and in studs if **all the
crowns face the same side** of the wall, so the siding plane stays true. More than ¼″, or
any twist you can rock on a flat bench, is not a part: recut it into a shorter piece the
cut list actually needs (cripples, rake studs, pier siding), or reject it. Quantities carry
no overage, so the honest cost of a bad board is a trip to the yard — which is why you
check boards in the yard, on the pile, before they are loaded.

## 2. Floor diagonals not equal

**Symptom.** The two tape readings corner-to-corner across the skid or floor rectangle
disagree by more than ½″.

**Likely cause.** One skid line slid along its length, the rims were nailed to joists cut or
set off layout, or the rectangle got shoved while the deck went on.

**Fix.** Measure both diagonals the same way, hook to hook. Push the corner at the end of
the **long** diagonal toward the short one until the two readings agree within ½″, then
re-nail and brace. Do this at the skids (Stage 1) and again at the deck — an out-of-square
floor telegraphs into every wall and the roof, and no later stage fixes it. If the readings
still disagree after the frame is eased, pull the corner nails and check the joist and rim
cuts against the list; a board cut more than ½″ off its length gets recut, not shimmed.

## 3. A wall that will not come plumb

**Symptom.** After raising, the level or plumb bob shows the wall leaning, and pulling the
braces will not bring it in — or it goes plumb at one end and out at the other.

**Likely cause.** In order of likelihood: the deck edge under the wall is not level, so the
plate follows the deck instead of the bubble; one crowned or long stud is holding the wall
off line; a brace is anchored to something that moves; the wall is twisted along its length
because its two ends are plumb to different marks.

**Fix.** Work from the ground up. Level the deck edge under the wall first and shim the
plate to follow it — never more than ½″ of shim. Then plumb off the **double top plate**,
not the studs, with the braces set at about 45° to solid deck blocking. A wall that is
plumb at both ends and bowed between has a bad stud: sight it, and recut or sister it.
Tolerance: no gap over ⅛″ under a 6-ft level laid on the wall face, in both directions,
before the next wall ties into it. The siding is the wall surface on this shed, so a racked
wall is a wavy wall you will see forever.

## 4. Rafter seats gapping on one plate

**Symptom.** A seated rafter shows a gap under one birdsmouth — usually the back seat —
while the other bears. A feeler or a saw blade slides in under the seat. The rafter may
rock when you push on the tail.

**Likely cause.** The birdsmouth was cut from the wrong line on the pattern rafter, the
pattern itself was cut off the plate height, or the plate it should bear on is high (a
double top plate lap that did not close, a wall left proud). One crowned rafter in thirteen
can also show this at one seat only.

**Fix.** First check the plates, not the rafter: both double top plate tops must be the
bearing lines, and a lap that did not close makes a high spot every rafter repeats. If the
plates are right, the rafter is wrong: recut the shallow seat or cut a new rafter from the
pattern. Do not wedge a shim into a structural seat and call it bearing. Tolerance: nothing
thicker than 1/16″ under either seat, checked rafter by rafter at Stage 8. The verify gate
on this model checks every seat face flat on its plate — the built shed gets the same
check, with a feeler.

## 5. Finish layers not sitting flush

**Symptom.** A finish layer — siding on framing, trim on siding, door planks on casing,
leaf frame on planks — rocks when you press it, or shows a wedge of daylight behind one
edge. Each layer is meant to back flat on the face of the one behind it, ¾″ proud, all the
way along.

**Likely cause.** The face behind is not in one plane: a crowned stud under the siding, a
siding course that did not seat in its rabbet, a casing nailed over a high corner board, or
a twisted board in the layer itself. The build-up is four ¾″ steps, so one high spot two
layers down shows as a rocking layer now.

**Fix.** Find the high spot before adding fasteners — press the board and read the gap,
then trace it back layer by layer to the first proud face. A crowned stud gets recut or
relieved; a siding course that did not nest gets lifted and re-set in its rabbet; a twisted
finish board gets replaced, primed stock is cheap compared to a wavy door. Tolerance: a
layer bears along its full length with no gap over 1/16″. Never spring a board flat with
screws — the stress lives in the joint forever and the fastener backs out.

## 6. Siding courses not matching across the rake

**Symptom.** On a side wall the top courses do not land parallel to the rake line: a course
pokes past the rake, or a wedge of gap opens between the top course and the rake board that
the frieze will not cover. Courses on the two side walls also fail to line up with each
other at the corners.

**Likely cause.** The wall's courses were started from a different mark than the other
walls (deck instead of skirt top, or a skipped course), so the 7″ exposure stack arrives at
the rake line off; or the rake-cut top course was cut square and left to stick out instead
of being ripped to the slope.

**Fix.** All four walls start their first course at the same mark — tucked behind the skirt
top — at the same 7″ exposure, so courses meet level at every corner board. Where the rake
crosses a course, that course is ripped or sloped to butt tight under the course above and
tight to the rake line; the frieze and casings cover a tight joint, not a wedge. If a wall
arrives at the rake a half-course off, do not stretch or squeeze the exposure to hide it —
pull that wall's courses back to the skirt and re-run them. Tolerance: a rake-cut course
sits tight to the rake board with no gap over ⅛″, and courses from adjacent walls meet
within ⅛″ at the corner.

## Before you move on

- [ ] The symptom that sent you here is gone, and the tool that checks it says so: tape, level, feeler, or story pole — not eye.
- [ ] Any part you recut was recut to a cut-list length, not trimmed until it "looked right."
- [ ] Nothing was shimmed past ½″, sprung flat with fasteners, or wedged into a bearing seat.
- [ ] If the honest call was a second trip to the yard, you made it.
